Dynamic tuning: long story short: informed historical tuning is never a mistake in itself, at worst it is redundant. Tuning e.g. the output gap / potential growth in distant history would probably have little effect on the current estimated value of the output gap, and hence on the current forecast, so the main advantage is (as you correctly suggest) is the historical behavior of the model, and calibration (mostly trend std-s).
For example, the Kalman filter may only capture a large drop in potential GDP is you set the trend growth std to a large value, which might be uncharacteristic of normal periods. In such a case the proper solution is to "draw the potential" by hand, i.e. tuning either the gap, or the trend growth, or their shocks, or any combination (a large std multiplier in the period of the drop could also help).
RER trend in R trend: when it comes to trend equation, there really is very little theoretical guidance concerning the form of these equations, simply because the trends are not theoretically well-defined economic concepts. The only restriction is that these equations must be consistent with a reasonable steady state, when all gaps are 0, inflation is on target, etc. Everything else is a decision of the modeler, based on their expert knowledge of the country and data. So if you are convinced that the inclusion of dl_z_tnd in r_tnd really leads to undue volatility in the policy rate, the you shouldn't shy away from modifying the equations to weaken or completely remove this relationship.
Output gap in the policy rule (responding also to your earlier question, better late then never):) I really do think that having the output gap in the policy rule and referring to this as a trade-off between output gap and inflation deviation is wrong.
The output gap appears in the original form of the Taylor-rule only because in the original article by Taylor the rule is a single equation, and Taylor used the output gap as a proxy for inflation expectations*. Once we put Taylor's idea in a model with model consistent expectations, there's no need to use a proxy. I'm pretty sure that the output gap did not appear in the policy rule of the original FPAS model; the policy rule there was even called an inflation-forecast based rule (IFBR), to avoid any misunderstanding. However, when the models were implemented in various countries, the modeling experts probably failed to properly explain all the implications of model consistent expectations, and gave in too easily to the demands of the modeling teams, which in most cases consisted of econometricians rather than modelers, who were used to working with single equations.
One consequence of model consistent expectations is that putting the output gap in the policy rule may even amplify the interest reaction to a demand shock, rather than mitigate it, as suggested by the term "trade-off", simply because expected inflation already increases after the (positive) demand shock. This endogenous nature of expectations is what proponents of the output-gap/inflation deviation trade-off completely miss.
Nevertheless, there might be reasonable roles for the output gap in the policy rule, for example, if we want to capture asymmetric policy reaction to domestic demand vs. other shocks. In this case a large positive coefficient on the output gap means that the CB reacts more aggressively to domestic demand shocks than to say premium shocks (even beyond what is implied by the difference in the expected inflation reaction). But this has nothing to do with the trade-off that "gap in the rule" afficionados usually advocate.
(*To be honest, I've never read the entire original Taylor article itself, so my argument above is based on discussions with OGR partners who were pretty close to the fire when the FPAS gap models were developed)
Regarding the UIP, it's proper interpretation is even more problematic and often misunderstood than the policy rule, but the main issue is the same: it does not make much sense to talk about UIP without specifying how FX expectations are formed, what they imply for model stability, and so on. Here I have much less expertise than with the policy rule (any argument involving lack of arbitrage is more finance than economics), so I would like to read the references that you sent before formulating my opinion/advice.

Exchange rate premia and UIP. This session gave an overview of state-of-the-art modeling of international forex markets with imperfections and uncertainty and presented new solutions to the old ER puzzles. One puzzle of relevance to the ER rule in the standard FPAS model is that the CIP and UIP (un/covered interest parity) deviations—i.e., forward or expected depreciation (forex/$) not being equal to the interest rate differential:  l_s{+1} – l_s  –  (i – i*) = - prem < 0, giving rise to a positive premium—not explained by country risk, but reflecting inter alia imperfections in arbitrage (market segmentation, bank regulation to limit risk exposure,…) and measurement problems.
One high-level theoretical paper by Itskhoki and Mukhin presented a general open economy framework, explaining all puzzles, incl. CIP-UIP deviations. Another empirical paper by IMF staff[1] analyzed drivers for CIP deviations for Emerging Market economies. In FPAS models for LICs like our QMFM, UIP has a small weight in the ER rule—and a large weight of the (gradual move to) the PPP equilibrium NER as driven by inflation differential and ERER change.[2] This also means that only a small part of the current account deficit is financed by short-term arbitrage capital flows responding to interest rate differences and forward or expected exchange rate changes. While for now the UIP debate is of lesser relevance to our QMFM, over time such flows may become more important and would lead to a larger weight of UIP in the ER rule.
